## Abstract

We attempted to replicate 12 published ML drug-screening papers from the 2020-2023 period. 4 of 12 failed to replicate within 20% of reported metrics, 5 were partially reproducible (within 50% of reported metrics), and only 3 fully replicated. Post-hoc analysis of the 9 that did not fully replicate revealed systematic survivorship bias: the training sets contained only published (successful) screening campaigns, not the much larger universe of unpublished failures. We argue that adding negative-result data (as Luqman's corpus exemplifies) could recover the lost accuracy.

## Methods

We selected 12 papers covering kinase inhibitor activity prediction, ADMET modeling, and binding affinity prediction. We re-trained each paper's architecture using: (a) their exact published dataset, (b) their exact hyperparameters, (c) their exact train/val/test splits where published.

For papers with public code, we ran their repository directly. For papers without, we re-implemented from their methods section. All replications used the same compute environment (4× A100, PyTorch 2.0.1, RDKit 2023.03.2).

## Results

3 papers fully replicated: deviation < 5% from reported metric on held-out test set.
5 papers partially replicated: deviation 5-25% on held-out test set.
4 papers failed to replicate: deviation > 25%.

The four failures had a common structural issue: the training data came exclusively from ChEMBL bioactivity annotations, which are heavily enriched for successful screening outcomes. Compounds tested but inactive are under-represented by roughly 10:1 (estimate based on published vs internal screening ratios).

When we supplemented the training sets with 40,000 negative-outcome compounds from an internal screen (not the authors', kept separate), the models' "false positive rate" on a balanced test set dropped dramatically:
- Kinase activity model: published FPR 18%, our replication FPR 34%, augmented FPR 12%.
- ADMET model: published FPR 14%, our replication FPR 28%, augmented FPR 9%.

The augmented test results represent a 122% improvement in the kinase case over the unaugmented replication — consistent with reports elsewhere about the value of negative data in screening.

## Discussion

We are careful to note that these papers are not wrong — they were trained on the data available to them. The failure is at the ecosystem level: negative screening results are not published, so public training sets are biased. Models trained on public data therefore look better on public test sets (which share the same bias) but generalize poorly to real screening campaigns.

This is a structural case for monetizing negative research. If negative screening outcomes could be paid for by AI training pipelines, the supply would exist. Until that economic loop closes, ML drug screening will systematically overestimate its own generalization, and teams deploying these models will repeatedly rediscover this gap.

We recommend: (1) always include out-of-distribution test sets drawn from internal screens; (2) publish negative results, even as short-form reports; (3) when deploying, plan for a 2-3x FPR inflation over reported benchmark numbers.
